Chapter 9

How Costs Are Calculated

One booking, seven numbers, and the exact order the app adds them up in — so you can check any invoice by hand.

What you'll be able to do

  • Turn a booking's start and end time into billable hours.
  • Work out an instrument's charge, whether it bills by the hour or by a typed-in amount.
  • Explain why 10 minutes of facility staff time can cost a full hour.
  • Apply a discount to the right part of the bill, and only the right part.
  • Follow overhead and tax through to the final total, in the order the app applies them.
  • Read every line of the app's own Cost & Time Breakdown panel and know where each number came from.

The running example

Every number on this page comes from one imagined booking. Keep it in mind as you read — each section adds one more piece to it.

💡 Tip. A researcher books the confocal microscope from 9:00 to 11:00 (2 hours), and asks a facility staff member to help set it up for the first 40 minutes of that window. The confocal costs 100/hour. The helper's rate is 95/hour. The researcher's lab has a standing 5% group discount. The facility's overhead is 10% internal + 5% external, and tax is 17%.

By the end of this chapter you'll see this booking come to a total of 383.47 — and know exactly why.

Step 1 — Booking hours

The app starts every calculation with one plain fact: how long was the instrument reserved for? That's simply the end time minus the start time, turned into hours.

  1. 9:00 to 11:00 is 2 hours exactly (11:00 − 9:00 = 2:00 → 2.0 hours).
  2. 9:00 to 11:30 would be 2.5 hours (2 hours 30 minutes → 2.5).
  3. If either time is left blank, or the end time is not after the start time, booking hours count as 0 — nothing is billed for time.

🧮 The math. Booking hours = (end time − start time), expressed as a decimal number of hours. Our example: 11:00 − 9:00 = 2.0 hours.

These are plain clock times on a single calendar day — see Local dates for why the app never has to worry about time zones here.

Step 2 — Instrument lines

Every instrument attached to a booking (see Cost & billed per) is set up to bill one of two ways:

  1. Billed per Time — the instrument's cost-per-hour is multiplied by the booking hours from Step 1.
  2. Billed per Unit / Weight / Other — the instrument's cost is multiplied by a separate amount you type in on the booking itself (samples run, grams of material, and so on) — booking hours don't come into it at all.

Our confocal is billed per Time, at 100/hour, for a 2-hour booking:

🧮 The math. Instrument line = cost-per-hour × booking hours = 100 × 2.0 = 200.

🔍 Why it works this way. Not every instrument's real cost tracks time — a mass spectrometer run might be billed per sample, a 3D printer per gram of filament. Letting each instrument choose "billed per" means the booking form asks for exactly the number that instrument actually needs, instead of forcing everything through an hourly rate that wouldn't make sense for it.

A booking can carry several instruments; each gets its own line the same way, and a multi-instrument booking occupies each instrument for the full booking window (this matters again in Reports).

Some facilities set up a pricing tier for a lab (see Step 6) that also gives one or more instruments a different rate just for labs on that tier. When the booking's lab has a tier assigned and that instrument has a rate set for it, this step uses the tier's rate here instead of the instrument's own listed cost-per-hour — everything else about the step works exactly the same way. A facility that never sets up tiers never sees this; every instrument simply bills its own listed rate.

Step 3 — Staff lines: the one-hour minimum

Facility staff (see Facility Staff vs. attendees) bill differently from instruments. Each staff member assigned to a booking can have their own start and end time inside the booking — helpful when someone only needs to be there for part of it. Leave their times blank and they're billed for the whole booking window instead.

Our helper worked the first 40 minutes of the 2-hour booking (9:00–9:40), at 95/hour:

  1. Raw hours: 9:40 − 9:00 = 40 minutes = 0.667 hours.
  2. Round up to a whole hour: anything above zero is rounded UP to the next whole hour — 0.667 rounds up to 1.
  3. Apply the one-hour floor: even a booking of a few minutes bills at least 1 hour. (For our 0.667, the round-up already lands on 1, so the floor doesn't change anything further — but see the box below for a case where it does.)
  4. Multiply by rate: 1 hour × 95/hour = 95.

🧮 The math. Billed hours = the larger of (1 hour) and (raw hours rounded up to the next whole hour). Staff line = rate × billed hours. Our example: 0.667 raw hours → 1 billed hour → 95/hour × 1 = 95.

💡 Tip — the classic example. A staff member helps for just 10 minutes. Raw hours = 0.167. Rounded up, that's still 1 hour — so 10 minutes of help bills as a full hour. Now say someone helps for 1 hour and 5 minutes (1.083 raw hours): rounding up takes that to 2 billed hours, not 1 — crossing any part of the hour boundary rounds up to the next one.

🔍 Why it works this way. When a staff member is booked to help, the facility reserves that person's time in advance — they can't pick up other work in the gaps of an oddly-shaped booking. Billing in whole hours, rounded up, reflects that the time was reserved, not just used down to the minute. It's the same reason a parking garage charges a flat rate for the first hour even if you're there 12 minutes.

A booking can have several staff members, each billed independently by their own window (or the whole booking, if left blank). Every staff line adds to the total the same way.

A facility can also set a Category (the booking's own Category field) to bill staff time at a percentage of the person's normal rate instead of the full rate — anywhere from 0% (that category's staff time is free) up to 100% (the normal rate, unchanged). That percentage applies after the one-hour floor above, and only to staff lines — it never touches an instrument charge. Our example booking uses a category with no such adjustment, so the helper's line stays exactly the 95 worked out above.

Step 4 — The subtotal

Add every instrument line and every staff line together:

🧮 The math. Subtotal = instrument line(s) + staff line(s) = 200 + 95 = 295.

Step 5 — Discounts (time-billed instrument money only)

A booking's discount percentage is two things added together, then capped:

  1. A standing group/lab discount set once in Settings for that lab (see Group Discounts).
  2. An optional manual discount an admin can add to one specific booking.
  3. The two are added together, but the combined percentage is capped at 100% — a discount can never make a line negative.

Our researcher's lab has a standing 5% discount and no manual override, so the combined rate is 5%.

⚠️ Careful — this is the part people get wrong. The discount is never applied to the whole subtotal. It applies only to the part of the bill that came from a time-billed instrument line (the 200 confocal charge in our example) — never to facility staff time, and never to per-unit or per-weight instrument charges. Our staff line of 95 is completely unaffected by the discount.

🧮 The math. Discount amount = time-billed instrument money × discount% = 200 × 5% = 10. Amount after discount = subtotal − discount amount = 295 − 10 = 285.

🔍 Why it works this way. A lab discount is meant to be a break on facility equipment pricing — a relationship the facility has negotiated for instrument time. Facility staff time reflects a real person's paid hours regardless of whose lab booked them, and per-unit charges (a consumable, a sample fee) are pass-through costs the facility doesn't mark up — neither is the discount's to give away.

A booking remembers the exact discount percentage it was saved with, so changing or revoking a lab's discount later never rewrites an old booking's total (see Cost snapshots below, and Billing Rates).

Step 6 — Overhead (applied after the discount)

The facility adds overhead on top of whatever is left after the discount. Exactly ONE overhead percentage applies to a given booking, decided by the booking's lab:

  1. If the lab has been assigned a named pricing tier, that tier's own single percentage is used, on its own — nothing else is added to it.
  2. If the lab has no tier assigned, the app falls back to the older rule: Internal overhead % plus External overhead % added together — two numbers left over from before pricing tiers existed. They can no longer be edited anywhere in Settings, and a facility set up today has both at zero, so an untiered lab simply gets no overhead. Assigning a pricing tier is now the only way to give a lab an overhead percentage.
  3. Either way, that one resolved percentage is applied to the post-discount amount, not the original subtotal.

Our researcher's lab has no pricing tier assigned, so the older fallback applies: Internal overhead 10% + External overhead 5% = a combined 15%.

🧮 The math. Overhead amount = post-discount amount × overhead% = 285 × 15% = 42.75. Amount before tax = 285 + 42.75 = 327.75.

🔍 Why it works this way. Internal and external overhead rates exist because many facilities charge a different overhead depending on whether the booking's lab is part of the same institution or an outside customer. Pricing tiers do the same job with a named rate a facility can set up per lab instead of only that internal/external split — useful once a facility has more than two overhead rates in practice (a subsidized-partner rate, say). A facility that never sets up a tier for any lab never sees this choice: every booking simply uses the Internal+External fallback, exactly as before tiers existed. See Billing Rates for where the fallback percentages are set.

Step 7 — Tax (on top of overhead — it compounds)

Tax is applied last, and it's applied to the amount that already includes overhead — not to the original subtotal. That means tax is effectively charged on the overhead too.

🧮 The math. Tax amount = amount-before-tax × tax% = 327.75 × 17% = 55.72 (327.75 × 0.17 = 55.7175, shown rounded to the cent). Total = 327.75 + 55.72 = 383.47.

🔍 Why it works this way. Overhead isn't a separate side charge — once it's added, it's simply part of what's being billed, the same way a restaurant bill's tax is calculated on top of a service charge rather than only on the food. Applying tax after overhead (rather than before it, or on the original subtotal) means the "amount before tax" figure the app shows is genuinely what the facility would invoice before any tax line — a real, checkable number, not an intermediate one.

Service entries use simpler math

Not every charge on a project comes from a booking. A service entry is a standalone billable line — technician time, sample prep, a per-unit charge — logged without an instrument session at all; see Service Entries for the full picture.

None of the seven steps on this page apply to one. A service entry's charge is simply Quantity × Rate, typed in directly — no discount, no overhead, and no tax step. Even so, it counts toward a project's cost exactly the way a booking's total does: it's its own row on the project's Project Costs card, and its total adds into the same running total shown there.

Why an old booking never changes

The total you see on a saved booking is a snapshot: every number above — hours, rates, discount %, overhead %, tax % — is frozen into that booking the moment it's saved.

🔍 Why it works this way. If the facility later raises its instrument rate, changes its overhead percentage, or a lab's discount is revoked, none of that should silently rewrite what was already billed and possibly already invoiced months ago. A cost snapshot means a booking from last year always shows exactly what it showed last year — see Cost snapshots for how this plays out across the rest of the app.

This is also why cancelling a booking doesn't simply zero out its cost, and why Reports can show retired staff and archived projects with their real historical charges intact.

Check yourself

A facility staff member is assigned to a booking for exactly 15 minutes at a rate of 80/hour. What does that staff line cost?

80. Fifteen minutes is above zero raw hours, so it's rounded up to 1 whole hour (the one-hour minimum/round-up rule), and 1 hour × 80/hour = 80 — the same as if they'd worked a full hour.

A booking has a 200 time-billed instrument charge and a 150 facility-staff charge, with a combined 20% discount. How much discount is actually applied?

40 (20% of 200) — not 20% of the 350 total. The discount only ever applies to the time-billed instrument money, never to staff time.

True or false: raising the tax rate in Settings next month will update the total on a booking from last month.

False. Every booking's total is a cost snapshot frozen at save time — changing rates, overhead, or tax afterward never rewrites an existing booking.
